A practical guide to water data

Understanding Your Water

Water affects our health, our ecosystems, and our communities. AquaNY brings public measurements into one place. This guide explains what those numbers actually mean—and what they don't.

Why water information matters

A river's condition changes with weather, runoff, seasons, and human activity. Drinking-water systems are tested for substances that may affect health over time. Public agencies collect this information, but the results can be difficult to find or interpret.

Understand local waterways

Water level and streamflow can help explain changing river conditions. Temperature, oxygen, and other measurements reveal aspects of aquatic habitat.

Recognize contaminants

Laboratory tests can identify chemicals such as PFAS, arsenic, or nitrate. Knowing what a substance is helps you ask better questions about a result.

Find the context

One measurement is a snapshot, not a complete verdict. Location, date, units, detection limits, and the type of sample all matter.

What do river measurements mean?

Not every measurement is a pollutant. Many describe the physical or chemical conditions at a USGS station. AquaNY only labels parameters it can identify reliably.

Streamflow

ft³/s

Measures how much water passes a location each second. Higher streamflow often occurs after rainfall or snowmelt and can indicate stronger currents or increased flood risk. There is no universal good or bad streamflow value; it depends on the river's normal conditions.

Gage height

ft

Measures water-surface height relative to a station's reference point. A rising gage height may indicate increasing flood risk, especially when approaching local flood-stage thresholds. The number alone does not tell you whether flooding will occur.

Water temperature

°F / °C

Affects aquatic organisms and how much oxygen water can hold. Warmer water generally holds less dissolved oxygen and can stress cold-water fish such as trout. Temperatures above 68°F (20°C) may be stressful for some cold-water species, but there is no single safe temperature for every waterbody.

Dissolved oxygen

mg/L

Measures oxygen available for fish and other aquatic life. Higher values are generally more favorable. Concentrations below 5 mg/L can stress many fish, while levels below 3 mg/L are particularly concerning. Different species have different oxygen requirements.

pH

unitless

Measures how acidic or basic water is. A pH of 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic, and above 7 is basic. A commonly used EPA freshwater aquatic-life reference range is 6.5–9.0. Values outside this range may stress organisms and affect how other chemicals behave in the water.

Turbidity

FNU / NTU

Measures cloudiness caused by suspended particles. Higher turbidity can reduce sunlight penetration and affect aquatic habitats. Sudden increases above a river's normal level may indicate runoff, sediment, or algae. There is no universal NTU or FNU cutoff that defines poor water quality for every river.

Specific conductance

µS/cm

Indicates how well water conducts electricity, which is related to its concentration of dissolved ions. Higher values may reflect more dissolved salts or minerals from road salt, runoff, or natural geology. Unusually high readings compared with a station's normal values may warrant investigation, but there is no universal harmful threshold.

Salinity

PSU

Measures dissolved salts in water. Freshwater generally has salinity below about 0.5 PSU, while seawater is around 35 PSU. Elevated salinity can harm freshwater organisms, although higher values are normal in tidal estuaries such as parts of the Hudson River.

Water-surface elevation

ft

Measures water elevation relative to a reference datum, such as NAVD88 or NGVD29. Higher elevations may result from tides, storms, or increased river levels. Whether a value is concerning depends on local flood thresholds and the reference datum, not the number alone.

What are the contaminants?

A contaminant is a substance measured in a water sample. Detection does not automatically mean a health standard was exceeded. Standards and interpretations depend on the substance, amount, source, and regulatory context.

PFAS: PFOA and PFOS

PFAS are a large family of manufactured chemicals used in industrial processes and consumer products. PFOA and PFOS are two widely studied examples. They can persist in the environment, and long-term exposure to certain PFAS has been associated with health concerns.

Often reported in ng/L (approximately parts per trillion in water)

Nitrate

A form of nitrogen that may enter water through fertilizers, wastewater, and natural processes. Elevated nitrate in drinking water is a particular concern for infants.

Check the reported chemical basis and units

Arsenic

A naturally occurring element that can enter groundwater from rocks and minerals. Long-term consumption of elevated concentrations in drinking water can pose health risks.

Often reported in µg/L

Other detected contaminants

Water-system summaries may also include disinfection byproducts, solvents, radionuclides, and other substances. AquaNY displays selected detected analytes, but its short card list is not the full monitoring history.

About PFAS standards

New York regulates PFOA and PFOS in public drinking water. Regulatory thresholds and compliance schedules can change. For current standards and official guidance, consult the New York State Department of Health rather than treating an AquaNY card as a compliance determination.

How to read a result responsibly

Check where

Is it a specific USGS station, an environmental sampling location, or a public water system? A nearby station may not be matched to the waterbody you searched.

Check when

Look at the observation timestamp or reporting period. “Latest available” can still be months or years old. Annual and quarterly summaries may overlap.

Check what

Read the parameter name, unit, and detection status. “Nondetect” means not detected under the applicable testing method—not necessarily zero.

What about mean and maximum?

In AquaNY's drinking-water cards, mean is a summary average and maximum is the highest summarized value for the selected reporting period. These are not necessarily individual tap samples. “No published measurement” is different from “Nondetect.”

Common questions

Does a detected contaminant mean my drinking water is unsafe?

Not necessarily. A detection tells you a substance was measured, not whether a regulatory threshold was exceeded or whether there is an immediate health risk. Check the water supplier's official report and public notices.

Can I drink or swim in a river based on AquaNY readings?

No. Streamflow, temperature, oxygen, pH, and historical pollutant sampling cannot establish current drinking or recreational safety. Consult local health advisories and official beach or waterbody notices.

Why does a station on the map have data while a river search shows none?

The station map includes individual USGS stations. The waterbody view shows readings only where stations have been confidently associated with a particular waterbody. Some nearby stations are not yet matched, and coverage is incomplete.

Why are some station measurements missing?

AquaNY displays only parameter codes it can identify reliably, and not every station records every parameter. Some stations also have no recent readings. Missing data does not prove that a substance or condition is absent.

Where can I find the latest information about my tap water?

Confirm your supplier using your water bill or local utility website, read its latest annual Consumer Confidence Report (water quality report), and check official advisories from your water supplier or health department.

What do ng/L, µg/L, and mg/L mean?

They mean nanograms, micrograms, and milligrams per liter. In water, 1 mg/L equals 1,000 µg/L, and 1 µg/L equals 1,000 ng/L. Always compare measurements using matching units and the correct contaminant-specific standard.
